Part A

Recognise

Units 1–3 · ~4 hours

Inborn errors of metabolism are individually rare and collectively common, and most children who die of one die before anyone says the word ‘metabolic’. This Part teaches the three habits that change that: notice the trajectory that does not fit, run the metabolic questions alongside sepsis rather than after it, and read the first bedside results as a pattern.

▸Unit 1 Could this be metabolic? Not attempted

Nobody diagnoses an inborn error they never considered. The skill here is not memorising hundreds of disorders; it is recognising the illness that does not behave like the diagnosis you gave it.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise the clinical trajectories that should prompt the question ‘could this be metabolic?’
  • UG Explain why a negative family history does not exclude an inherited disorder.
  • UG/PG Use the three mechanistic groups — intoxication, energy deficiency, complex molecules — to organise a differential.
  • PG Hold two explanations open when sepsis and a metabolic disorder may coexist.
Your role
  • Student. Recognise the pattern, describe it clearly and escalate to the supervising clinician.
  • PG resident. Keep the metabolic differential open alongside sepsis, organise first-line tests and call the metabolic team early.
  • Faculty. Assess whether learners can state the trajectory and the finding their diagnosis explains least well, not whether they can name rare enzymes.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Begin with the trajectory Must-know

Ask one question of every sick child: does the course fit the working diagnosis? Consider an inborn error of metabolism (IEM) when you see:

  • A well interval, then deterioration — a newborn who fed normally for days, then became sleepy and stopped feeding.
  • Encephalopathy out of proportion to the fever, dehydration or infection.
  • Recurrent episodes of vomiting, drowsiness or ataxia with illness or fasting, and a child who is well between them.
  • Several organ systems becoming involved without an obvious unifying cause: brain, liver, heart, muscle, kidney.
  • Loss of skills the child once had.
Pearl

Vomiting alone is common. Vomiting with altered consciousness and an unusual biochemical pattern deserves a different level of attention.

2 · Three groups that lead to action Must-know

GroupMechanismTypical storyExamples
IntoxicationA toxic small molecule accumulatesSymptom-free interval, then acute decompensation with feeding or illnessUrea cycle disorders, organic acidaemias, MSUD, galactosaemia
Energy deficiencyCells cannot make or use fuelHypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, cardiomyopathy, myopathy; acute or progressiveFatty-acid oxidation disorders, glycogen storage disease, mitochondrial disease
Complex moleculesLarge molecules cannot be built or broken downSlowly progressive, multisystem; organomegaly, coarse features, regressionLysosomal, peroxisomal and glycosylation disorders

The groups organise the differential and point to urgency: intoxication disorders are emergencies where hours matter. They are a teaching map, not a formal classification, and some disorders overlap.

3 · Take a family story without blame Must-know

Ask about unexplained childhood deaths, siblings with similar illness, miscarriages and parental relatedness (consanguinity). Many affected children have no recognised family history: most IEMs are autosomal recessive, and carriers are well. Say: “Inherited conditions can occur even when everyone else in the family is well.”

The error clinicians actually make

“No family history, so unlikely.” Replace it with what you actually asked and found. A first affected child in a family with no known history is the usual situation, not the exception.

4 · Keep two explanations open Must-know UG/PG

A positive infection result may explain only part of the picture. Classic galactosaemia can present with jaundice, liver dysfunction, bleeding and E. coli sepsis; hyperammonaemia can be missed in a baby being treated for sepsis. Treat the infection and ask which finding your diagnosis explains least well.

India lens

Consanguineous marriage is common in several Indian communities and raises the chance of autosomal recessive disorders. Ask about it respectfully and record it as information, never as blame. Newborn screening coverage is uneven across states (Unit 17), so many Indian children with an IEM present clinically, not through a screen.

Why the answer is what it is

Recurrent illness-related encephalopathy is a classic metabolic trajectory. The current episode is the best opportunity for diagnosis: assess and stabilise urgently, check glucose and ketones, and send the first-line metabolic tests while treating the illness.

A — most IEMs are autosomal recessive; a negative family history is usual. B — waiting wastes the diagnostic window and risks harm. D — the history raises a possibility; it does not make a diagnosis.
The best time to diagnose an episodic metabolic disorder is during the episode.

▸Unit 2 The sick neonate Not attempted

The newborn has only a few ways to show illness: poor feeding, sleepiness, abnormal breathing, seizures. Sepsis, heart disease, surgical problems and inborn errors all look alike at first, so they must be investigated together.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise the newborn who was well and then deteriorates.
  • UG Start the first-line metabolic tests in parallel with the sepsis work-up.
  • UG/PG Explain why a pending or normal newborn screen does not reassure in a symptomatic baby.
  • PG Recognise neonatal presentations of urea cycle disorders, organic acidaemias and MSUD.
Your role
  • Student. Recognise the well-then-sick timeline, call for help and assist with observation and documentation.
  • PG resident. Send ammonia, glucose, gas, lactate and ketones with the sepsis screen, and escalate an abnormal result the same hour.
  • Faculty. Run the case as a simulation in which the ammonia result is the trigger; debrief on how long it took the team to ask for it.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · A well start can precede illness Must-know

Fictional case: Aarav fed normally after an uncomplicated birth. On day 4 he has a weak suck, increasing sleepiness and fast breathing. In intoxication disorders, the placenta clears the toxic metabolite before birth; once feeds begin, it accumulates. A symptom-free interval of hours to days is a clue to keep, not proof.

Write the timeline
  Birth → normal feeds → first change → now
  day 0        days 1–3       day 4          day 4, 6 h later
  well         feeding well   weak suck      drowsy, fast breathing

2 · Assess the baby before the label Must-know

Airway, breathing, circulation, neurological state, temperature and glucose come first. A newborn who is hard to wake, feeds poorly or breathes abnormally needs prompt senior review. Fever may be absent in serious infection. Keep sepsis, congenital heart disease (duct-dependent lesions), surgical causes and endocrine emergencies (adrenal crisis) in the same differential.

Danger

Never wait for antibiotics to “fail” before checking ammonia. A baby with unexplained encephalopathy needs an ammonia result within the hour.

3 · Send the first tests in parallel Must-know

TestWhy it matters now
Glucose (bedside, confirmed in lab)Treat hypoglycaemia immediately; it also shapes the differential
Blood gas with anion gap and lactateAcidosis versus respiratory alkalosis points to different disease groups
AmmoniaThe single most important time-critical test in an encephalopathic newborn
Ketones (blood or urine)Ketosis in a newborn is unusual and suggests an organic acidaemia or MSUD
Blood count, electrolytes, liver function, clotting, cultureSepsis, liver failure and cytopenias guide both pathways

Store extra plasma and urine for specialised tests (Unit 5). Do not delay stabilisation for a complete set.

4 · Do not wait for a signature sign Must-know UG/PG

MSUD causes feeding difficulty then encephalopathy; the maple-syrup odour is often absent or noticed late. Many classic signs — odour, cataracts, dysmorphism — are late or missing. Urgency comes from the baby’s state, not from a hallmark. A newborn screening result that is pending, reported normal or never done does not exclude an IEM in a sick baby.

Ideal setting
  • Ammonia, gas, lactate and ketones available 24 hours, with results in under an hour
  • Direct phone line to a metabolic consultant and PICU
Resource-constrained setting
  • If ammonia is not available on site at night, send it urgently to the nearest laboratory that can run it and call the referral centre at the same time
  • Start glucose-containing fluids and stop protein feeds while waiting (Unit 4) if a metabolic crisis is suspected
  • Transfer early: a sick newborn with suspected hyperammonaemia is safer on the road at 250 µmol/L than at 800

Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 6-day-old with poor feeding and increasing drowsiness has a newborn screen reported as normal on day 3. What does this mean?

Nudge

What can a normal screen not exclude?

Structured hint

Screening panels differ, some disorders present before results, and false negatives occur. What does the baby need now?

Why the answer is what it is

A normal screen lowers the probability only of disorders on that panel, and false negatives occur. Many disorders are not screened. A symptomatic baby needs clinical assessment and first-line metabolic tests now.

A — no screen excludes every IEM. C — repeating the screen delays urgent tests. D — waiting is unsafe in a deteriorating newborn.
Screening changes probability; it never replaces assessment of a sick baby.

▸Unit 3 Read the metabolic pattern Not attempted

Five bedside results — glucose, ketones, pH with anion gap, lactate and ammonia — point to a disease family long before a specialised test returns. Reading them together is the most useful skill in this module.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Combine acid–base state, glucose, ketones, lactate and ammonia into one physiological story.
  • UG Calculate the anion gap and interpret it.
  • UG/PG Name a provisional disease family from the pattern, without declaring a diagnosis.
  • PG Identify results that need urgent repetition or laboratory discussion.
Your role
  • Student. Calculate the anion gap and describe the pattern in words.
  • PG resident. Match the pattern to a disease family and choose the next discriminating test.
  • Faculty. Give learners the five results as cards and ask for a provisional map with one explicit uncertainty.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · The anion gap Must-know

Anion gap
  Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)
  Typical reference range about 8–16 mmol/L (check your laboratory;
  some include K⁺ and use a higher range)

  Raised gap   → unmeasured acids: lactate, ketones, organic acids,
                 toxins, renal failure
  Normal gap   → bicarbonate loss (diarrhoea) or renal tubular acidosis

The anion gap reflects unmeasured anions, not a disease. Interpret it with the laboratory’s method and reference interval, and with albumin (a low albumin lowers the gap).

2 · Five patterns worth knowing Must-know

PatternThink first of
High ammonia, respiratory alkalosis, no acidosis, normal glucoseUrea cycle disorder
Raised-gap metabolic acidosis, ketosis, ± high ammonia, low platelets or neutrophilsOrganic acidaemia (methylmalonic, propionic, isovaleric)
Encephalopathy, ketosis, little or no acidosis, normal ammoniaMaple syrup urine disease (MSUD)
Hypoglycaemia with low or absent ketonesFatty-acid oxidation disorder or hyperinsulinism
Hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, large liverGlycogen storage disease type I or gluconeogenesis defect
Persistent raised lactate with normal perfusionMitochondrial or pyruvate metabolism disorder

Patterns overlap, and a very sick baby can show several at once. Each pattern narrows the differential; none confirms an enzyme defect.

3 · Low glucose with too few ketones Must-know

When glucose is low after fasting or illness, the body should be burning fat and making ketones. Inappropriately low ketones mean fat is not being used (fatty-acid oxidation disorder) or insulin is switching fat-burning off (hyperinsulinism). “Hypoketotic” does not mean zero: interpret the ketone level against how low the glucose is and for how long. Urine ketones being present does not exclude a fatty-acid oxidation disorder.

4 · Check context before closing the case Must-know UG/PG

Lactate rises with poor perfusion, seizures, a struggling child or a tourniquet. Ammonia rises falsely with a difficult sample, delayed processing or haemolysis, and truly in liver failure, some drugs (valproate) and sepsis. When a result is implausible, repeat it quickly — do not dismiss it. An ammonia that is high on a poor sample is still a reason to act while the repeat is done.

Pearl

Write three columns: pattern → plausible mechanisms → next discriminating test. Revisit it after each result instead of forcing new values to fit the first idea.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

A 4-day-old is drowsy and breathing fast. Blood gas: pH 7.49, PCO₂ 27 mmHg, bicarbonate 20 mmol/L. Glucose is normal and ammonia is markedly raised. Which disease family is most likely?

Nudge

Is this an acidosis or an alkalosis, and is it metabolic or respiratory?

Structured hint

High ammonia stimulates breathing. Which disease family raises ammonia without causing acidosis?

Why the answer is what it is

High pH with low PCO₂ is a respiratory alkalosis; ammonia drives hyperventilation. High ammonia without acidosis or hypoglycaemia points first to a urea cycle disorder. Confirmation needs plasma amino acids and urine orotic acid.

A — organic acidaemias usually cause a raised-gap metabolic acidosis with ketosis. B — fatty-acid oxidation disorders usually present with hypoketotic hypoglycaemia. C — glycogen storage disease causes hypoglycaemia with lactic acidosis.
Ammonia with alkalosis: think urea cycle. Ammonia with acidosis and ketones: think organic acid.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 20-month-old is found drowsy after a night of vomiting. Glucose is 1.9 mmol/L and blood ketones are 0.3 mmol/L. What does this pattern most suggest?

Nudge

What should happen to ketones when a child has been fasting and the glucose is low?

Structured hint

Ketones of 0.3 mmol/L with glucose 1.9 mmol/L after a night of vomiting are far too low. Which two problems stop fat being turned into ketones?

Why the answer is what it is

After fasting with glucose this low, ketones should be high. Inappropriately low ketones mean fat is not being used (fatty-acid oxidation disorder such as MCAD deficiency) or insulin is suppressing it (hyperinsulinism). Treat the hypoglycaemia first and take a critical sample if possible.

A — a normal fasting response makes ketones. B — ketotic hypoglycaemia has high ketones. D — ketones here are low, not high.
Read glucose and ketones together; neither means much alone.
