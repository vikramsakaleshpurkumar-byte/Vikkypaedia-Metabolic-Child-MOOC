Part C

Presentations by system

Units 9–13 · ~7 hours

Outside the neonatal crisis, inborn errors usually arrive through one organ: weak muscles, a large liver, seizures that will not stop, abnormal movements, or a child who is losing skills. This Part starts with that organ and asks which metabolic questions it should raise — especially the ones with treatments.

▸Unit 9 Muscle, heart and fatty-acid oxidation Not attempted

Fasting, fever and exercise are stress tests of the body's fuel supply. A child who collapses, gets rhabdomyolysis or develops cardiomyopathy under that stress may have a fatty-acid oxidation disorder.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise presentations of fatty-acid oxidation disorders (FAODs) at different ages.
  • UG Link fasting, fever and exercise triggers to fuel failure.
  • UG/PG Assess heart, muscle and kidneys together in an acute episode.
  • PG Recognise infantile Pompe disease as a weak baby with a large heart.
Your role
  • Student. Ask about the last meal, fever, exercise and dark urine in any child with weakness or collapse.
  • PG resident. Send the acute sample, check CK, renal function and echo, and avoid IV lipid until FAOD is excluded.
  • Faculty. Teach through a sudden unexpected death case: what questions would have changed the outcome for the next sibling?

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Triggers reveal the fuel problem Must-know

  • Infancy: hypoketotic hypoglycaemia, encephalopathy, liver dysfunction, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, sudden death, often after a fast or intercurrent illness.
  • MCAD deficiency (the commonest FAOD in many populations): previously well child, becomes drowsy with vomiting after poor intake; hypoketotic hypoglycaemia; can be fatal in the first episode.
  • Long-chain disorders (VLCAD, LCHAD, CPT2): cardiomyopathy and liver disease in infants; exercise- or illness-induced muscle pain and rhabdomyolysis in older children.
Pearl

LCHAD deficiency in the fetus is associated with maternal acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome. Ask the mother.

2 · The acute episode Must-know

CheckWhy
Glucose and ketonesHypoketotic hypoglycaemia
CK, urine myoglobin, potassium, creatinineRhabdomyolysis threatens the kidneys and heart
ECG and echocardiogramCardiomyopathy and arrhythmia
Liver function, ammoniaLiver involvement
Acylcarnitine profile, urine organic acidsDiagnostic during the episode; can normalise afterwards
Danger

Treat with IV glucose at a rate that stops fat breakdown. Do not give IV lipid to a child with a suspected long-chain FAOD. Give generous fluids for rhabdomyolysis and watch potassium.

3 · A weak baby with a large heart Must-know

Infantile-onset Pompe disease (a lysosomal glycogen storage disorder): hypotonia, weakness, feeding and breathing difficulty, large tongue, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, often with a short PR interval and huge QRS complexes on ECG. CK is often raised. Enzyme assay (dried blood spot) and genetic testing confirm it. Enzyme replacement therapy works best when started early, so a delay of months matters.

4 · Prevention is individual Must-know UG/PG

Long-term care avoids fasting, provides a sick-day plan, and for some long-chain disorders uses special fat formulas; the plan depends on the exact disorder, age and heart. Families need a written emergency letter.

India lens

Sudden unexplained infant deaths are rarely investigated in India. If a sibling died suddenly after a brief illness, ask about it, and consider acylcarnitine testing of the living child. Pompe disease is one of the rare diseases for which enzyme replacement may be supported under the National Policy for Rare Diseases through Centres of Excellence (Unit 19); refer early.

Why the answer is what it is

Recurrent rhabdomyolysis triggered by prolonged exercise, fasting or fever suggests a long-chain FAOD, commonly CPT2 deficiency or VLCAD deficiency. Treat the rhabdomyolysis (fluids, potassium, renal function) and send acylcarnitines.

A — recurrent rhabdomyolysis with fever is not simple strain. B — MCAD deficiency typically causes hypoketotic hypoglycaemia, not rhabdomyolysis. D — Duchenne causes progressive weakness and constantly high CK, not episodic triggered attacks.
Recurrent rhabdomyolysis is a metabolic question.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 3-month-old is floppy, feeds slowly and breathes fast. He has a large tongue. The chest X-ray shows a large heart and the ECG shows a short PR interval with very large QRS complexes. What should be arranged?

Nudge

Structured hint

Large tongue, short PR and huge QRS complexes point to one lysosomal disorder. How is it confirmed quickly?

Hypotonia with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, macroglossia and a short PR interval suggests infantile Pompe disease. A dried blood spot enzyme assay and genetic testing confirm it; early enzyme replacement changes outcomes.

▸Unit 10 The liver Not attempted

Galactosaemia, hereditary fructose intolerance and tyrosinaemia type I all injure the liver, and all have treatments. The history of what the child has eaten, and when, is often the diagnosis.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise metabolic red flags in neonatal and infant liver disease.
  • UG Link the timing of lactose, fructose or sucrose exposure to symptoms.
  • UG/PG Know the urgent first steps for suspected galactosaemia and tyrosinaemia type I.
  • PG Recognise Wilson disease in the older child with liver and neurological signs.
Your role
  • Student. Take a precise feeding history: breast milk, formula, first fruit, sugar and sweet medicines.
  • PG resident. Stop the suspected sugar under specialist advice, treat sepsis and coagulopathy, and send the confirmatory tests.
  • Faculty. Assess whether learners ask what the child ate before they order a liver panel.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Read the liver phenotype Must-know

IEMs cause neonatal cholestasis, acute liver failure, hepatomegaly, coagulopathy out of proportion to jaundice, and renal tubular dysfunction. Ask about feed type, when fruit, juice or sugar started, sweet medicines (sucrose or sorbitol syrups), vomiting after feeds, hypoglycaemia, and aversion to sweets.

2 · Three diet-linked disorders Must-know

DisorderWhenCluesFirst steps
Classic galactosaemiaDays after milk feeds begin (breast or formula)Vomiting, jaundice, liver failure, bleeding, E. coli sepsis, cataractsStop lactose (lactose-free formula) under specialist advice; treat sepsis; GALT enzyme assay (before any transfusion)
Hereditary fructose intoleranceWhen fruit, sucrose or sorbitol enters the dietVomiting, hypoglycaemia, liver and kidney dysfunction, aversion to sweetsRemove fructose, sucrose and sorbitol; genetic testing. Never do a fructose challenge
Tyrosinaemia type IInfancy (acute liver failure) or later (rickets, liver disease)Coagulopathy with modest jaundice, renal Fanconi syndrome, rickets, neurological crises; cancer riskSuccinylacetone in blood or urine; nitisinone and diet via the metabolic team
The error clinicians actually make

Sending the GALT enzyme after a blood transfusion. Donor red cells give a falsely normal result for weeks; store a pre-transfusion sample or use genetic testing.

3 · The older child: Wilson disease Must-know

Wilson disease can present from about 3–5 years with unexplained raised transaminases, hepatitis, acute liver failure with haemolysis, or later with deteriorating handwriting, tremor, dystonia, behavioural change or falling school performance. Kayser–Fleischer rings are often absent in children with liver presentation. Diagnosis combines ceruloplasmin, 24-hour urine copper, liver copper and genetics; no single test is enough. Treatment works, and siblings must be screened.

Danger

Acute liver failure with Coombs-negative haemolysis in a child or teenager: think Wilson disease and contact a liver transplant centre at once.

4 · Act within a shared pathway Must-know UG/PG

Urgent liver care (vitamin K, glucose, infection treatment, liver unit referral) and diagnostic confirmation proceed together. Give the family a precise interim feeding plan from the treating team; broad unguided eliminations can cause malnutrition.

India lens

Soy-based or lactose-free formulas are widely available in Indian cities but may be costly or absent in rural areas. Nitisinone for tyrosinaemia type I is expensive; check support through the National Policy for Rare Diseases and Centres of Excellence (Unit 19). Wilson disease is relatively frequently diagnosed in Indian liver and neurology clinics — include it early in any unexplained liver disease after infancy.

Liver dysfunction, coagulopathy and E. coli sepsis after milk feeds suggest classic galactosaemia. Stop lactose (breast milk and standard formula) under specialist advice, take a GALT sample before any transfusion, and continue sepsis care.

A — continuing lactose worsens the liver failure. C — a newborn on milk has not yet been exposed to fructose. D — this is not simple jaundice.

Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 7-month-old has had vomiting, poor growth and a large liver since starting fruit purée and sweetened cereal at 6 months. She now refuses sweet foods. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what should be avoided?

Why the answer is what it is

Symptoms that start when fructose and sucrose enter the diet, with aversion to sweets, suggest hereditary fructose intolerance. Remove fructose, sucrose and sorbitol (including sweet medicines) and confirm genetically. A fructose challenge can cause severe hypoglycaemia and liver failure.

B — galactosaemia presents in the first weeks of milk feeding. C — milk allergy does not explain a link with fruit. D — coeliac disease does not cause aversion to sweets or this timing with fruit.

▸Unit 11 Seizures Not attempted

Most seizures are not metabolic. A few metabolic epilepsies have specific treatments, and missing them leaves a child on a growing list of antiseizure drugs that do not work.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise seizure patterns that should prompt a metabolic question.
  • UG Check glucose, calcium, magnesium and sodium in every seizing child.
  • UG/PG Know the monitored pyridoxine trial and the other vitamin-responsive epilepsies.
  • PG Plan paired CSF and blood sampling before a lumbar puncture.
Your role
  • Student. Check glucose and electrolytes in every seizing child and describe the seizure pattern.
  • PG resident. Consider pyridoxine, pyridoxal phosphate, biotin and GLUT1 deficiency in early refractory seizures, and store samples before treatment trials.
  • Faculty. Use a refractory neonatal seizure case to teach that treatment trials need monitoring and pre-trial samples.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · When seizures become a metabolic question Must-know

  • Neonatal or early infantile seizures that do not respond to first- and second-line drugs.
  • Seizures with encephalopathy, acidosis, hypoglycaemia or hyperammonaemia.
  • Seizures related to fasting, with developmental regression, or with a movement disorder.
  • A family history of neonatal seizures or death.

First exclude the common: hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia, infection, hypoxic–ischaemic injury and structural lesions.

2 · Vitamin-responsive epilepsies Must-know

DisorderTreatment trial (under monitoring, per protocol)
Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy (ALDH7A1)Pyridoxine 100 mg IV with EEG and cardiorespiratory monitoring; repeat up to 500 mg if no response. Non-acute: 30 mg/kg/day orally for 3–5 days (GeneReviews)
Pyridoxal-phosphate-responsive epilepsy (PNPO)Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate orally, per specialist protocol
Biotinidase deficiencyBiotin 5–10 mg/day orally (profound deficiency, GeneReviews 2026)
Cerebral folate deficiencyFolinic acid, per specialist protocol
Danger

Give the first IV pyridoxine dose with resuscitation ready: some infants with pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy develop apnoea and cardiorespiratory depression after it. Take plasma and urine for biomarkers (alpha-AASA, pipecolic acid) before the trial when possible, but do not delay treatment of status epilepticus to do so.

3 · Other metabolic epilepsies Must-know

DisorderClueTest
GLUT1 deficiencySeizures before meals, paroxysmal movements, acquired microcephalyLow CSF glucose with a paired blood glucose (CSF:blood ratio usually below about 0.45); treated with ketogenic diet
Glycine encephalopathy (non-ketotic hyperglycinaemia)Neonatal encephalopathy, hiccups, apnoea, burst-suppression EEGPaired CSF and plasma glycine
Serine deficiencyCongenital microcephaly, seizuresLow CSF and plasma serine (fasting)
Creatine deficiencySpeech delay, intellectual disability, seizuresUrine and plasma creatine metabolites; brain MR spectroscopy

4 · Plan samples before the lumbar puncture Must-know UG/PG

CSF glucose, lactate, glycine and neurotransmitters need a paired blood sample taken just before the LP, specific tubes and fraction order, and often immediate freezing. Contact the laboratory first; a CSF sample for neurotransmitters cannot be repeated easily.

India lens

Specialised CSF studies usually go to a handful of referral laboratories. If they are not available, a monitored pyridoxine trial and genetic testing (epilepsy gene panels are increasingly affordable in India) may give the answer. Biotin is cheap and widely available; a child with seizures, alopecia and rash deserves a biotinidase assay.

Checkpoint 2 of 2

Why the answer is what it is

Seizures before meals, paroxysmal ataxia and falling head growth suggest GLUT1 deficiency. A low CSF glucose with a normal paired blood glucose (ratio usually below about 0.45) supports it; genetics confirms it. The ketogenic diet is the treatment.

A — ammonia does not diagnose GLUT1 deficiency. B — reducing substances relate to galactosaemia. D — sweat chloride is for cystic fibrosis.

▸Unit 12 Movement disorders Not attempted

Describe the movement before naming the disease. Dystonia, chorea, ataxia and parkinsonism in a child point to different metabolic pathways — and several of them have treatments.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Describe paediatric movement disorders accurately.
  • UG Recognise Wilson disease and glutaric aciduria type 1 as treatable causes.
  • UG/PG Recognise fluctuating dystonia and oculogyric crises as clues to neurotransmitter disorders.
  • PG Plan CSF neurotransmitter sampling correctly.
Your role
  • Student. Describe the movement in words and, with consent, record a video.
  • PG resident. Link the movement to associated liver, eye, skin or imaging clues and choose the pathway to test.
  • Faculty. Use short consented videos for learners to name the movement before discussing causes.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Name the movement Must-know

MovementWhat it looks like
DystoniaSustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing twisting postures
ChoreaBrief, irregular, flowing movements moving from one body part to another
AtaxiaUnsteady, poorly coordinated movement and gait
ParkinsonismSlowness, rigidity, reduced facial expression, tremor
Oculogyric crisisEpisodes of sustained upward eye deviation

Ask whether it is constant or episodic, worse later in the day, triggered by illness, and whether skills have been lost.

2 · Treatable causes not to miss Must-know

DisorderCluesWhy it matters
Wilson diseaseOlder child or teenager; dystonia, tremor, dysarthria, behaviour change; liver disease; Kayser–Fleischer rings (usual in neurological presentation)Chelation or zinc treatment works; untreated it is fatal
Glutaric aciduria type 1Large head (macrocephaly) in infancy, then sudden dystonia after an illness; wide Sylvian fissures on imagingBrain injury can be prevented by diet and an emergency regimen if diagnosed before the first crisis
Dopa-responsive dystoniaDystonia worse in the evening, improving after sleepDramatic response to low-dose levodopa
Biotin–thiamine-responsive basal ganglia diseaseSubacute encephalopathy, dystonia, basal ganglia changes after a feverResponds to biotin and thiamine if given early
The error clinicians actually make

Labelling a child with dystonia after a febrile illness as “encephalitis” or “cerebral palsy” without checking urine organic acids (GA1) or trying biotin and thiamine.

3 · Neurotransmitter disorders Must-know

Disorders of dopamine and serotonin production (e.g. AADC deficiency, tyrosine hydroxylase deficiency) cause hypotonia, oculogyric crises, dystonia, autonomic symptoms (sweating, nasal congestion, temperature instability) and developmental delay, often with daily fluctuation. Diagnosis needs CSF neurotransmitter metabolites and genetics.

4 · CSF has a strict protocol Must-know UG/PG

CSF neurotransmitters need specific fractions in order, no blood contamination, immediate freezing and dry-ice transport. Interpretation uses age-specific reference ranges and medication history. Never improvise the collection; plan it with the laboratory.

India lens

Many children with metabolic movement disorders in India carry a label of cerebral palsy. Reassess when there was no clear perinatal injury, the course is progressive or fluctuating, or the imaging does not fit. A levodopa trial for suspected dopa-responsive dystonia is cheap, safe under supervision and can be transformative.

▸Unit 13 Developmental delay and regression Not attempted

Most developmental delay is not metabolic. Regression — losing skills once gained — changes that, as do associated clues in the eyes, skin, bones, liver or behaviour.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Distinguish developmental delay from regression.
  • UG Recognise red flags that raise the yield of metabolic testing.
  • UG/PG Know the treatable metabolic causes of intellectual disability: PKU, homocystinuria, creatine and biotin disorders, others.
  • PG Build a tiered, affordable evaluation with neurology and genetics.
Your role
  • Student. Establish whether skills were never gained or were lost, with examples and dates.
  • PG resident. Look for red flags and send a targeted first-tier panel for treatable causes.
  • Faculty. Teach that developmental support and diagnostic work run together; neither waits for the other.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Delay or regression? Must-know

Delay is slow acquisition of skills. Regression is loss of skills the child had reliably: walking, words, feeding, school work. Confirm with caregivers using specific examples and videos. Regression, episodic worsening, a movement disorder, seizures, eye or skeletal changes, organomegaly or thrombosis raise the chance of an IEM.

The error clinicians actually make

An old label such as “cerebral palsy” or “autism” stops people looking again. When the course changes, reassess.

2 · Treatable causes Must-know

DisorderCluesFirst test
Phenylketonuria (untreated)Fair hair and skin, eczema, musty odour, intellectual disability, seizuresPlasma phenylalanine
Classic homocystinuriaLens dislocation, tall thin build, thrombosis, intellectual disabilityPlasma total homocysteine
Creatine deficiency syndromesSevere speech delay, behaviour problems, seizuresUrine creatine and guanidinoacetate; MR spectroscopy
Biotinidase deficiencySeizures, hypotonia, alopecia, rash, hearing lossBiotinidase enzyme
Congenital hypothyroidism (not an IEM, but a must)Prolonged jaundice, constipation, poor growthTSH and free T4

Other clues: self-injury with high uric acid (Lesch–Nyhan syndrome); regression with organomegaly or coarse features (lysosomal disorders, Unit 15).

3 · Phenylketonuria Must-know

Untreated classic PKU causes severe intellectual disability; early dietary treatment prevents it, which is why PKU was the first disorder screened in newborns. Current European and American guidelines aim to keep blood phenylalanine at 120–360 µmol/L in children, with a phenylalanine-restricted diet and protein substitute, lifelong. Some children respond to sapropterin (BH4). Women with PKU need tight control before and during pregnancy to prevent maternal PKU syndrome in the baby.

4 · A tiered evaluation Must-know UG/PG

Start with history, examination, growth, hearing and vision, thyroid function, and a first-tier panel for treatable causes chosen with neurology and genetics: plasma amino acids, total homocysteine, urine organic acids, acylcarnitines, and creatine metabolites where indicated. Genomic testing (exome) now has a high yield in unexplained delay and may be the most efficient next step. Early intervention and rehabilitation start now, not when the diagnosis arrives.

India lens

Newborn screening for PKU is not universal in India, so untreated PKU still presents with intellectual disability and seizures. Phenylalanine-free protein substitutes are expensive and not always available; ask the Centre of Excellence and patient support groups about access.
