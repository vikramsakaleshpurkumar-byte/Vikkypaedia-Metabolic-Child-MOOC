Part A

Recognise

Units 1–3 · ~4 hours

Inborn errors of metabolism are individually rare and collectively common, and most children who die of one die before anyone says the word ‘metabolic’. This Part teaches the three habits that change that: notice the trajectory that does not fit, run the metabolic questions alongside sepsis rather than after it, and read the first bedside results as a pattern.

▸Unit 1 Could this be metabolic? Not attempted

Nobody diagnoses an inborn error they never considered. The skill here is not memorising hundreds of disorders; it is recognising the illness that does not behave like the diagnosis you gave it.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise the clinical trajectories that should prompt the question ‘could this be metabolic?’
  • UG Explain why a negative family history does not exclude an inherited disorder.
  • UG/PG Use the three mechanistic groups — intoxication, energy deficiency, complex molecules — to organise a differential.
  • PG Hold two explanations open when sepsis and a metabolic disorder may coexist.
Your role
  • Student. Recognise the pattern, describe it clearly and escalate to the supervising clinician.
  • PG resident. Keep the metabolic differential open alongside sepsis, organise first-line tests and call the metabolic team early.
  • Faculty. Assess whether learners can state the trajectory and the finding their diagnosis explains least well, not whether they can name rare enzymes.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Begin with the trajectory Must-know

Ask one question of every sick child: does the course fit the working diagnosis? Consider an inborn error of metabolism (IEM) when you see:

  • A well interval, then deterioration — a newborn who fed normally for days, then became sleepy and stopped feeding.
  • Encephalopathy out of proportion to the fever, dehydration or infection.
  • Recurrent episodes of vomiting, drowsiness or ataxia with illness or fasting, and a child who is well between them.
  • Several organ systems becoming involved without an obvious unifying cause: brain, liver, heart, muscle, kidney.
  • Loss of skills the child once had.
Pearl

Vomiting alone is common. Vomiting with altered consciousness and an unusual biochemical pattern deserves a different level of attention.

2 · Three groups that lead to action Must-know

GroupMechanismTypical storyExamples
IntoxicationA toxic small molecule accumulatesSymptom-free interval, then acute decompensation with feeding or illnessUrea cycle disorders, organic acidaemias, MSUD, galactosaemia
Energy deficiencyCells cannot make or use fuelHypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, cardiomyopathy, myopathy; acute or progressiveFatty-acid oxidation disorders, glycogen storage disease, mitochondrial disease
Complex moleculesLarge molecules cannot be built or broken downSlowly progressive, multisystem; organomegaly, coarse features, regressionLysosomal, peroxisomal and glycosylation disorders

The groups organise the differential and point to urgency: intoxication disorders are emergencies where hours matter. They are a teaching map, not a formal classification, and some disorders overlap.

3 · Take a family story without blame Must-know

Ask about unexplained childhood deaths, siblings with similar illness, miscarriages and parental relatedness (consanguinity). Many affected children have no recognised family history: most IEMs are autosomal recessive, and carriers are well. Say: “Inherited conditions can occur even when everyone else in the family is well.”

The error clinicians actually make

“No family history, so unlikely.” Replace it with what you actually asked and found. A first affected child in a family with no known history is the usual situation, not the exception.

4 · Keep two explanations open Must-know UG/PG

A positive infection result may explain only part of the picture. Classic galactosaemia can present with jaundice, liver dysfunction, bleeding and E. coli sepsis; hyperammonaemia can be missed in a baby being treated for sepsis. Treat the infection and ask which finding your diagnosis explains least well.

India lens

Consanguineous marriage is common in several Indian communities and raises the chance of autosomal recessive disorders. Ask about it respectfully and record it as information, never as blame. Newborn screening coverage is uneven across states (Unit 17), so many Indian children with an IEM present clinically, not through a screen.

▸Unit 2 The sick neonate Not attempted

The newborn has only a few ways to show illness: poor feeding, sleepiness, abnormal breathing, seizures. Sepsis, heart disease, surgical problems and inborn errors all look alike at first, so they must be investigated together.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise the newborn who was well and then deteriorates.
  • UG Start the first-line metabolic tests in parallel with the sepsis work-up.
  • UG/PG Explain why a pending or normal newborn screen does not reassure in a symptomatic baby.
  • PG Recognise neonatal presentations of urea cycle disorders, organic acidaemias and MSUD.
Your role
  • Student. Recognise the well-then-sick timeline, call for help and assist with observation and documentation.
  • PG resident. Send ammonia, glucose, gas, lactate and ketones with the sepsis screen, and escalate an abnormal result the same hour.
  • Faculty. Run the case as a simulation in which the ammonia result is the trigger; debrief on how long it took the team to ask for it.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · A well start can precede illness Must-know

Fictional case: Aarav fed normally after an uncomplicated birth. On day 4 he has a weak suck, increasing sleepiness and fast breathing. In intoxication disorders, the placenta clears the toxic metabolite before birth; once feeds begin, it accumulates. A symptom-free interval of hours to days is a clue to keep, not proof.

Write the timeline
  Birth → normal feeds → first change → now
  day 0        days 1–3       day 4          day 4, 6 h later
  well         feeding well   weak suck      drowsy, fast breathing

2 · Assess the baby before the label Must-know

Airway, breathing, circulation, neurological state, temperature and glucose come first. A newborn who is hard to wake, feeds poorly or breathes abnormally needs prompt senior review. Fever may be absent in serious infection. Keep sepsis, congenital heart disease (duct-dependent lesions), surgical causes and endocrine emergencies (adrenal crisis) in the same differential.

Danger

Never wait for antibiotics to “fail” before checking ammonia. A baby with unexplained encephalopathy needs an ammonia result within the hour.

3 · Send the first tests in parallel Must-know

TestWhy it matters now
Glucose (bedside, confirmed in lab)Treat hypoglycaemia immediately; it also shapes the differential
Blood gas with anion gap and lactateAcidosis versus respiratory alkalosis points to different disease groups
AmmoniaThe single most important time-critical test in an encephalopathic newborn
Ketones (blood or urine)Ketosis in a newborn is unusual and suggests an organic acidaemia or MSUD
Blood count, electrolytes, liver function, clotting, cultureSepsis, liver failure and cytopenias guide both pathways

Store extra plasma and urine for specialised tests (Unit 5). Do not delay stabilisation for a complete set.

4 · Do not wait for a signature sign Must-know UG/PG

MSUD causes feeding difficulty then encephalopathy; the maple-syrup odour is often absent or noticed late. Many classic signs — odour, cataracts, dysmorphism — are late or missing. Urgency comes from the baby’s state, not from a hallmark. A newborn screening result that is pending, reported normal or never done does not exclude an IEM in a sick baby.

Ideal setting
  • Ammonia, gas, lactate and ketones available 24 hours, with results in under an hour
  • Direct phone line to a metabolic consultant and PICU
Resource-constrained setting
  • If ammonia is not available on site at night, send it urgently to the nearest laboratory that can run it and call the referral centre at the same time
  • Start glucose-containing fluids and stop protein feeds while waiting (Unit 4) if a metabolic crisis is suspected
  • Transfer early: a sick newborn with suspected hyperammonaemia is safer on the road at 250 µmol/L than at 800

▸Unit 3 Read the metabolic pattern Not attempted

Five bedside results — glucose, ketones, pH with anion gap, lactate and ammonia — point to a disease family long before a specialised test returns. Reading them together is the most useful skill in this module.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Combine acid–base state, glucose, ketones, lactate and ammonia into one physiological story.
  • UG Calculate the anion gap and interpret it.
  • UG/PG Name a provisional disease family from the pattern, without declaring a diagnosis.
  • PG Identify results that need urgent repetition or laboratory discussion.
Your role
  • Student. Calculate the anion gap and describe the pattern in words.
  • PG resident. Match the pattern to a disease family and choose the next discriminating test.
  • Faculty. Give learners the five results as cards and ask for a provisional map with one explicit uncertainty.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · The anion gap Must-know

Anion gap
  Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)
  Typical reference range about 8–16 mmol/L (check your laboratory;
  some include K⁺ and use a higher range)

  Raised gap   → unmeasured acids: lactate, ketones, organic acids,
                 toxins, renal failure
  Normal gap   → bicarbonate loss (diarrhoea) or renal tubular acidosis

The anion gap reflects unmeasured anions, not a disease. Interpret it with the laboratory’s method and reference interval, and with albumin (a low albumin lowers the gap).

2 · Five patterns worth knowing Must-know

PatternThink first of
High ammonia, respiratory alkalosis, no acidosis, normal glucoseUrea cycle disorder
Raised-gap metabolic acidosis, ketosis, ± high ammonia, low platelets or neutrophilsOrganic acidaemia (methylmalonic, propionic, isovaleric)
Encephalopathy, ketosis, little or no acidosis, normal ammoniaMaple syrup urine disease (MSUD)
Hypoglycaemia with low or absent ketonesFatty-acid oxidation disorder or hyperinsulinism
Hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, large liverGlycogen storage disease type I or gluconeogenesis defect
Persistent raised lactate with normal perfusionMitochondrial or pyruvate metabolism disorder

Patterns overlap, and a very sick baby can show several at once. Each pattern narrows the differential; none confirms an enzyme defect.

3 · Low glucose with too few ketones Must-know

When glucose is low after fasting or illness, the body should be burning fat and making ketones. Inappropriately low ketones mean fat is not being used (fatty-acid oxidation disorder) or insulin is switching fat-burning off (hyperinsulinism). “Hypoketotic” does not mean zero: interpret the ketone level against how low the glucose is and for how long. Urine ketones being present does not exclude a fatty-acid oxidation disorder.

4 · Check context before closing the case Must-know UG/PG

Lactate rises with poor perfusion, seizures, a struggling child or a tourniquet. Ammonia rises falsely with a difficult sample, delayed processing or haemolysis, and truly in liver failure, some drugs (valproate) and sepsis. When a result is implausible, repeat it quickly — do not dismiss it. An ammonia that is high on a poor sample is still a reason to act while the repeat is done.

Pearl

Write three columns: pattern → plausible mechanisms → next discriminating test. Revisit it after each result instead of forcing new values to fit the first idea.

A 4-day-old is drowsy and breathing fast. Blood gas: pH 7.49, PCO₂ 27 mmHg, bicarbonate 20 mmol/L. Glucose is normal and ammonia is markedly raised. Which disease family is most likely?

Is this an acidosis or an alkalosis, and is it metabolic or respiratory?

Structured hint

High ammonia stimulates breathing. Which disease family raises ammonia without causing acidosis?

Why the answer is what it is

High pH with low PCO₂ is a respiratory alkalosis; ammonia drives hyperventilation. High ammonia without acidosis or hypoglycaemia points first to a urea cycle disorder. Confirmation needs plasma amino acids and urine orotic acid.

A — organic acidaemias usually cause a raised-gap metabolic acidosis with ketosis. B — fatty-acid oxidation disorders usually present with hypoketotic hypoglycaemia. C — glycogen storage disease causes hypoglycaemia with lactic acidosis.
Ammonia with alkalosis: think urea cycle. Ammonia with acidosis and ketones: think organic acid.

Nudge

What should happen to ketones when a child has been fasting and the glucose is low?

Structured hint

Ketones of 0.3 mmol/L with glucose 1.9 mmol/L after a night of vomiting are far too low. Which two problems stop fat being turned into ketones?

Why the answer is what it is

After fasting with glucose this low, ketones should be high. Inappropriately low ketones mean fat is not being used (fatty-acid oxidation disorder such as MCAD deficiency) or insulin is suppressing it (hyperinsulinism). Treat the hypoglycaemia first and take a critical sample if possible.

A — a normal fasting response makes ketones. B — ketotic hypoglycaemia has high ketones. D — ketones here are low, not high.

Part B

The first hour

Units 4–8 · ~7 hours

In a metabolic crisis the diagnosis usually arrives days later; the outcome is often decided in the first hour. This Part is about what to do before you know: stop the toxic load, give glucose, capture the sample that will make the diagnosis, and get the ammonia down.

▸Unit 4 The first hour Not attempted

When a metabolic crisis is suspected, four things happen together: resuscitate, stop the fuel for the toxin, give enough glucose to stop the body breaking itself down, and call for expert help.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Name the concurrent first-hour priorities in a suspected metabolic crisis.
  • UG Start glucose-containing fluid at a rate that reverses catabolism.
  • UG/PG Explain why protein is stopped for a short time only, and why lipid is not given until a fatty-acid oxidation disorder is excluded.
  • PG Give a complete, closed-loop referral to the metabolic and intensive-care team.
Your role
  • Student. Call for help, check glucose, and help with monitoring and documentation as directed.
  • PG resident. Lead the first hour: resuscitation, glucose infusion, stopping feeds, sampling, and the call to the metabolic team.
  • Faculty. Time the scenario: when did glucose start, when was ammonia sent, when was the metabolic team called?

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any treatment beyond your training, local protocol and specialist advice.

1 · Four things at once Must-know

Suspected metabolic crisis: the first hour
  RESUSCITATE     airway, breathing, circulation; treat seizures and shock
  STOP            protein and milk feeds (and lactose/fructose if suspected)
  GLUCOSE         10% glucose IV at a rate that stops catabolism
                  (e.g. 5 mL/kg/h ≈ 8 mg/kg/min in a baby — BIMDG example)
  SAMPLE          ammonia, gas, lactate, ketones, glucose + stored plasma
                  and urine (Unit 5) — do not delay treatment for them
  CALL            metabolic team and PICU/NICU early; plan transfer
  REASSESS        glucose, ammonia, gas and neurology every 1–4 hours

2 · Why glucose, and how much Must-know

In illness and fasting the body breaks down its own muscle protein and fat for fuel. In an intoxication disorder that catabolism produces more of the toxin; in a fat-oxidation disorder the fat cannot be used. Enough glucose switches catabolism off. A maintenance-strength drip is often not enough.

  • Treat hypoglycaemia first: 10% glucose 2 mL/kg (200 mg/kg) IV.
  • Then 10% glucose with appropriate electrolytes; the BIMDG undiagnosed-hyperammonaemia guideline uses 5 mL/kg/h in a baby (about 8 mg/kg/min). Watch fluid balance and cerebral oedema risk, and adjust to the child and the protocol.
  • If glucose rises above the target range, the specialist team may add insulin rather than reduce glucose.
The error clinicians actually make

Running 0.9% saline or a maintenance-strength drip “until we know”. It rehydrates without stopping catabolism, and in intoxication disorders the toxin keeps rising.

3 · Protein, lipid and the emergency letter Must-know

  • Stop protein when an intoxication disorder is suspected, but for a short time: prolonged protein restriction (more than 24–48 hours) causes the body to break down its own protein. The metabolic team reintroduces it.
  • Do not give IV lipid until a fatty-acid oxidation disorder has been excluded; in other disorders the team may add it for calories.
  • Known diagnosis? Find the child’s emergency letter or regimen, confirm it is current, and follow it. Families often carry it; ask.

4 · Close the communication loop Must-know UG/PG

A useful call says: who the child is; current airway, breathing, circulation and neurological state; timed results with units; what has been given and when; and what you need (advice, drugs, transfer, dialysis). Read back the plan and name who owns each task. A referral is incomplete until somebody accepts responsibility.

Ideal setting
  • On-site metabolic team, PICU, dialysis and specialist drugs
  • Ammonia, gas and lactate repeated every 2–4 hours
Resource-constrained setting
  • Start glucose and stop feeds before transfer; do not wait for confirmation
  • Phone the referral centre early; ask which specialist drugs they want started and whether you hold them
  • Send stored plasma and urine with the child, labelled with collection times and what treatment preceded them

▸Unit 5 Send the right sample Not attempted

The biochemistry during a crisis is often the only chance to make a diagnosis; hours later, after glucose, it may look normal. A critical sample taken in the first minutes can save a child years of uncertainty.

  • UG Collect ammonia correctly and recognise sources of false results.

  • PG Document timing and treatment so that results can be interpreted.

2 · Ammonia: a sample that lies easily Must-know

  • Haemolysis, delay, a warm sample and a squeezed heel-prick all raise the value falsely.
  • Call ahead so the laboratory knows it is urgent, and name who receives the result.

“It was probably a bad sample.” Repeat it immediately — but act on a high value in a sick child while you wait for the repeat.

4 · Label, document, track Must-know UG/PG

Each sample needs the time taken, the child’s state (well, fasting, in crisis) and what treatment came before it (glucose, carnitine, blood transfusion, feeds). Profiles can normalise after treatment; a normal report taken after 24 hours of glucose does not erase a compelling episode.

India lens

Specialised tests are often sent to a referral laboratory by courier. Agree in advance the container, volume, freezing and transport, and whether the laboratory accepts dried blood spots (which travel well and are cheap). Many state and private laboratories accept filter-paper spots for acylcarnitines and amino acids. Keep a laminated sample card in the emergency room and NICU.

Danger

OTC deficiency is X-linked: girls can be affected, and mothers may have been symptomatic without a diagnosis.

India lens

A — 1 in 4 applies to autosomal recessive disorders. C — each pregnancy carries the same risk. D — boys are more often and more severely affected.
Counsel from the actual inheritance pattern, not a default number.

2 · Prepare for illness before it happens Must-know

Every child at risk of decompensation needs:

  • A written sick-day plan: when to start the emergency regimen at home, how often to give it, and when to come to hospital (vomiting, refusing the regimen, drowsiness, or no improvement after a set time).
  • An emergency letter for the hospital: diagnosis, first-hour treatment, drug doses, what to avoid, and a 24-hour contact.
  • A copy with the family, on their phone, and at the local hospital.
The error clinicians actually make

A child with a known disorder waits four hours in triage because nobody read the letter. Flag known metabolic patients in your hospital record so they are seen immediately.

3 · Look beyond the laboratory value Must-know

Each review: growth (weight, length, head circumference), dietary intake and supplies, biochemistry, development and school, bone health, and the family’s burden. A child can have perfect phenylalanine levels and be failing to thrive because the protein substitute is not being taken, or can be struggling at school with good biochemistry.

4 · School, surgery and adulthood Must-know UG/PG

  • School: a short letter on meals, snacks, sick days and emergency contacts.
  • Surgery and anaesthesia: fasting is dangerous; plan with the metabolic team for IV glucose from the start of the fast, and avoid drugs that are contraindicated (e.g. propofol-containing lipid in long-chain FAOD, per specialist advice).
  • Transition: teenagers need to learn their own diagnosis, diet and emergency plan; plan transfer to adult metabolic care and discuss pregnancy planning (maternal PKU).

PhaseTimeSay
Setting the scene1 min“We're here to learn, not to judge. What's said here stays here. I believe everyone did their best with the information they had.”

Three analysis methods, chosen by what you are addressing:

  • Plus–delta — for performance that was broadly good. “What worked? What would you change?” Fast, safe, low yield.
  • Advocacy–inquiry — for a specific puzzling action. State your observation and your reasoning, then ask theirs: “I noticed the saline was continued after the ammonia came back high. I was concerned because saline does not stop catabolism.   • Directive feedback with teaching — for a knowledge gap. Do not use inquiry to extract a fact somebody does not have; that is humiliating. Say the fact, explain the mechanism, move on.
Pearl — debrief the system, not only the people

At least one of your two or three analysis points should be about the environment: where the 10% glucose and the ammonia tubes were, how long help took, whether the clock was visible. C3 · Feedback structures

R2C2 — for longer, formal feedback
  1. Relationship — establish respect and genuine interest. “How is the rotation going for you?”
  2. Reaction — explore their response to the data before interpreting it. “What's your reaction to this feedback?”
  3. Content — confirm shared understanding of what the assessment actually says.
  4. Coaching — co-create a specific plan with a date. “What would you like to work on before we meet in six weeks?”
Ask–Tell–Ask — for brief, in-the-moment feedback

Ask: “How do you think that went?” · Tell: one specific observation and one specific suggestion · Ask: “What will you do differently next time?” Ninety seconds, at the warmer, immediately afterwards.

C5 · Standard setting — set your own cut score before you use this for anything consequential

Why this matters more than you think

Published comparisons of standard-setting methods applied to the same assessment have produced cut scores ranging from roughly 66% to 86%. That means the method you choose can matter more than how your candidates perform. A cut score chosen by preference — including the 80% this module ships with — is the weakest defensible option available. Do one of the exercises below with three to six judges before the module carries any consequence.

Modified Angoff worksheet
  1. Define the borderline candidate in writing, as a person: “a first-year resident who would reliably send ammonia in a sleepy newborn, start 10% glucose, take the critical sample and call the metabolic team, but would hesitate over scavenger doses or dialysis thresholds.” Judges must agree this before seeing any item.
  2. For each item, each judge independently estimates: out of 100 borderline candidates, how many would answer this correctly? Record as a percentage.
  3. Discuss items where judges differ by more than 20 points. Do not force consensus; discuss, then re-rate independently.
  4. The cut score is the mean of the judges' mean estimates.
  5. Record the standard error and consider whether to adjust for it. Document everything.
Ebel worksheet

Classify each item on two axes, then assign an expected percentage-correct to each cell.

 EasyMediumHard
Essential—%—%—%
Important—%—%—%
Acceptable—%—%—%

Cut score = Σ(items in cell × expected % for that cell) ÷ total items. Ebel is useful here because the module already tiers content as must / good / nice-to-know, which maps onto essential / important / acceptable.

Hofstee — a sanity check on whatever you produce

Judges agree four values: the minimum and maximum acceptable cut score, and the minimum and maximum acceptable failure rate. Plot the cumulative score distribution and find where the line crosses the rectangle those four values define. If your Angoff or Ebel cut falls outside that rectangle, something is wrong — either with the standard or with the assessment. Hofstee will not give you a defensible cut on its own, but it will stop you shipping an indefensible one.

Borderline regression, for the OSCE

For each station, examiners record both a checklist score and a global rating (fail / borderline / pass / good pass). Regress checklist score on global rating; the cut score is the checklist score predicted by a global rating of “borderline”. This is more defensible than a fixed percentage because it is anchored in expert judgement of these candidates on this station, and it generates the data as a by-product of examining.

G3 · Where this design is weaker than it looks

Published honestly, because a module claiming to be evidence-governed must be willing to
  1. Fixed intervals, not fitted forgetting curves. The Leitner schedule (1-3-7-21-60) is a crude approximation. A genuinely adaptive system would fit each learner's forgetting curve per item. This does not, and cannot, without collecting the data it deliberately does not collect. That is a real trade-off, not a free lunch.
  2. Thin item sampling per unit. Two checkpoint items cannot reliably sample a unit's content. Mastery of a unit here means “answered these two specific items correctly”, which is a weaker claim than it appears. The final assessment partially compensates; it does not eliminate the problem.
  3. Rule-based placement, not adaptive testing. The three placement questions set defaults by simple rules. They are not a measurement, and they do not adjust as evidence accumulates about what the learner actually knows.
  4. Limited form-to-form equivalence. Attempts sample 50 items from a pool of 70 with randomised order, so two attempts are not statistically equated. A score of 88% on one attempt is not strictly the same as 88% on another.
  5. Unproctored assessment. Nothing prevents a learner opening the module in another tab, or working with a colleague. The module is honest about this: it is a self-assessment with a certificate attached, and any high-stakes use requires proctoring and the skills assessment in Appendices A and B.
  6. The verification code is not tamper-proof. It is a deterministic hash of the displayed figures, re-derivable by anyone with the same inputs. It helps detect transcription error. It is not a security feature and is described as such on the certificate.
  7. The cut score is provisional and was chosen by preference. Appendix C exists because this is the weakest defensible option. Until your institution does the standard setting, treat the pass/fail line as an approximation.
  8. Kirkpatrick levels 3 and 4 are unmeasured. This module has no evidence yet that it changes time to diagnosis or child outcomes. Nor does almost any comparable resource — but the absence should be stated rather than implied away.
  9. Accessibility is targeted, not audited. WCAG 2.2 AA conformance has not been independently verified, and has not been tested with real screen-reader users. Automated checks and manual keyboard testing are not the same as a user audit.
  10. Clinical content has not been externally peer reviewed. It is written to the current primary guidelines and cross-checked, but it carries no editorial board and no formal review process. Corrections are welcomed and should be treated as expected rather than exceptional — see CONTRIBUTING.
  11. India-specific epidemiology moves. The figures quoted are from the most recent sources available at build; verify against the current SRS bulletin and national cause-of-death estimates before quoting them in teaching.
  12. One author, one perspective. Every choice about what is must-know versus nice-to-know reflects a single clinician's judgement about what matters at a district hospital at 2 a.m. Reasonable educators would tier some of it differently.
